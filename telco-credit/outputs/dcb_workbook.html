Part one

What counts as a default

Nobody in this data was ever given spendable credit, so the label cannot be "did not repay the DCB debt". It is the operator's own action: the month after the draw, was the line two-way barred.

The draw month sits between the features and the label on purpose. A subscriber is scored on what they did before the credit existed and judged on the first bill that carries it, which is the only shape a one-month product can be measured in.

The label is

  • sbrp_stat_id = 4 in month 140408 — a two-way bar. The line can neither call nor be called.
  • Two-way, not one-way. A one-way bar restricts telco spending, and DCB credit is a separate pool: a subscriber cannot buy packages with it and cannot pay their bill with it. A DCB default cannot produce a one-way bar, so counting one would be counting the wrong event.
  • One month. The draw is repaid on the next bill, so the window that matters is the one month after it.
  • Measured at 0.2208 pct, 19,215 events — thick enough to fit on. The four-month version of the same label ran 0.5213 pct.

The label is not

  • A repayment record. No subscriber in this window had DCB credit to fail to repay. The bar is a proxy for the behaviour that would cause a default, not an observation of one.
  • Prompt. A bar can lag more than the window allows, so some defaults land outside it and the measured rate is a floor.
  • Blind to substitution. Whether a subscriber spends DCB credit instead of what they already buy, or on top of it, is unknown. Substitution can only make this safer.
  • Behaviour under credit. Everything here is how subscribers behave without it. A pilot is the only thing that answers that.

Part two

Nine subscribers, worked through

Each card is a constructed profile. The six months of revenue are invented to make a point; every multiplier attached to them is measured, and the query it came from is printed under it.

What these cards do not contain is a model score. The model has not yet been refit on the one-month label, so there is no pd for these profiles and inventing one would be the worst thing on this page. What each card shows instead is arithmetic you can check: the measured base rate of 0.2208 pct multiplied by the measured premiums the profile triggers. Where two premiums apply, the range runs from the largest alone to all of them multiplied — the drivers are not known to be independent, and the truth is somewhere inside. Once the refit runs, every card gets a real pd and this arithmetic becomes the check on it.

Part three

Bill shock, and the two answers that were wrong

The question is narrow: a subscriber's bill jumps because a draw landed on it, so does the jump itself make them more likely to be barred. It took four passes to answer, and the first two answers were both wrong for reasons worth keeping.

Pass one — the raw gradient

Bar rate by how far the bill moved, across all subscribers, 140401–140406.

Read the top row before the gradient. The highest rate of any band belongs to subscribers whose bill fell, which is the opposite of what a shock story predicts. It was almost missed, because the first version of this query put the falling months inside the reference band and compared everything against them.

Pass two — compare each subscriber against themselves

A subscriber whose bill jumps may simply be a riskier subscriber. The fix is to use each person as their own control: one normal month, one shocked month, 1,169,169 pairs.

Why pass two could never have worked

Opposite directions, both strongly significant, and the pooled 0.97 was these two cancelling. The cause is structural. With two months per subscriber, a raised month in one position is a normal month in the other, so "raised against normal" is always also "140405 against 140406" — and those two months are judged on different outcome windows. 140406 runs 1.33 to 1.38 times hotter whichever role it plays. The design was never identified; no amount of data would have fixed it.

Withdrawn: "premium 1.00, measured on 1,169,169 paired comparisons". It had already been written into the deck and had to come back out.

Pass three — hold the calendar still instead

Every row is bill month 140406, so no month effect can survive. What is controlled instead is who gets a raised bill: the comparison is made inside narrow strata of how large the bill normally is and which way it was already moving.

the bill rose by up to 2× the bill rose by 2× or more

A large rise matters, and it matters most to subscribers whose bill was already climbing. For someone whose bill had been falling, a doubled month is a return to normal and carries no excess risk at all; for someone already on the way up it runs 1.668. The risk is in sustained escalation, not in one large bill. These figures are still understated: they were measured before the reference band was cleaned.

Pass four — the corrected run

Two defects found in the query above, both biasing the premiums down.

The reference band held the worst cases

  • The control was shock < 1.25, which swallowed every month where the bill fell — the riskiest months in the data, at 0.4116 pct against a normal 0.2290 pct.
  • Fixed to 0.80–1.25, with the falling months kept visible as a band of their own.

A missing value was treated as a stratum

  • prior_trend IS NULL means the first baseline month billed zero: a line that started or restarted inside the window, not a direction.
  • 109,360 of them, barring at 2.706 pct against 0.350 pct for everyone else, and 73,789 sat in the high-shock band because a bill appearing out of nothing reads as an enormous rise.

Mantel-Haenszel pooled over four bill-size bands and three prior-direction bands, one calendar month, new lines excluded. 12,042 defaults over 4.0 million subscriber-months. Crude and adjusted agree to three decimals (1.908 against 1.907) — adjusting for month, bill size and prior direction moved the estimate from 1.97 to 1.91, essentially not at all. That is itself the finding: the obvious confounders are not where the risk is. It does not prove causation; selection on something unobserved is still possible. The earlier interval of 0.30 to 12.09 was wrong, from summing per-stratum variances instead of using the Robins-Breslow-Greenland formula.

Part four

What the model is, and what it is not yet

Everything below was fitted on the four-month label. It ranks well and it is honestly calibrated at the level. It has not been refit for the one-month product, and until it is, the pd column is not the DCB pd.

ModelFirst runAfter adding bar historyGain
Logistic, VALID0.73850.7843+0.0458
HistGB, VALID0.79180.8157+0.0239
HistGB calibrated, TEST0.80170.8278+0.0261
The linear model gained twice what boosting did, because a prior bar is close to a straight additive signal. Removing the six pre_ columns costs 0.0239 of test AUC, which is 92 percent of what the refit bought. A scale-free variant — every Rial amount removed from the inputs — scores 0.8230, costing 0.0048. Take that trade: scale-free features cannot drift with inflation, and twelve features in the full model already drift significantly.

Calibration is conservative exactly where the book is drawn

Decile groupPredictedObservedRatio
Safest 3 deciles0.1038 pct0.0806 pct0.776
Safest 5 deciles0.1305 pct0.1171 pct0.898
All 10 deciles0.5232 pct0.5146 pct0.983
Level error is 1.68 percent, which is tight for a 0.5 percent event. But the model over-predicts risk in the safest deciles, which is where the book comes from. The error runs in the safe direction, so realised loss should land at or under the quoted figure — state it that way rather than quoting a point estimate. Observed risk is monotone across all ten deciles, so the ranking, which is what the book is cut on, is sound. The live set scores a mean pd of 0.5952 pct against TEST's observed 0.5146 pct, +15.7 percent: that is drift showing up in the level, not the ranking. Trust the order, not the number.

Part five

What is still open

Listed so that nothing on this page is read as more settled than it is.

01

The model has not been refit on the one-month label

42_model_datasets.sql now builds the DCB calendar and the one-month rate is measured, but the notebook has not been run against it. Until it is, every pd on the deck is a four-month pd and the scenario cards here carry arithmetic instead of scores.

02

Nobody in the data ever held credit

The largest unknown on the page, and the only thing that answers it is a capped pilot. A one-month term means the answer arrives in weeks rather than a year.

03

Capping the draw below one month's bill shrinks limits for light billers

The rule is right on risk and the volume consequence is unmeasured. It needs the billing distribution of the scored set, which is a query rather than an assumption.

04

The substitution share

Whether DCB spending replaces what a subscriber already buys or adds to it is unknown, and it decides whether the bill actually rises by the full draw. Everything on this page assumes it adds, which is the pessimistic reading.
