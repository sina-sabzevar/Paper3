Lending customers money on their next phone bill

A customer borrows up to 300,000 Toman, spends it on services they already buy, and repays it on the bill that arrives next month. This page is who can have it, how much each person should get, and what it is likely to cost us. No forecasts — every number here is something we counted.

A one-month loan is the whole reason the second and third figures are so different. Money lent in month one comes back in month two and goes out again in month three. A four-month loan does that three times a year; this does it twelve times.

The first check does almost all of the work. It removes 31,182,117 customers, which is 97 out of every 100 we turn away. It is not a judgement about whether they would repay — it is about whether their bill is big enough to carry a loan at all. If we want more customers, that number is the one to move, and moving it adds volume without making the book riskier.

How much to give each person

One rule covers it, and it is the most useful thing we measured.

Never lend someone more than they bill in a month.
If the loan is smaller than their usual bill, the bill they get goes up by less than double, and customers in that position stop paying at close to the normal rate. Once the loan is as large as the bill, the bill doubles — and customers whose bill doubles stop paying almost twice as often. The line is sharp, and it sits exactly at "the loan equals the bill".

What it is likely to cost

Worked out in the open, so every step can be argued with.

1

9,344,723 customers borrow 300,000 Toman each

That is 2,803 bn Toman out on loan at any one time, and 33,641 bn lent over a full year as the same money cycles twelve times.

2

About 2.2 in every 1,000 do not pay it back

That is what we counted on customers who passed the same three checks, over one month, which is the length of the loan. The real figure for the lending book should be lower, because we would be turning away everyone carrying a warning sign.

3

So we lose roughly 74.3 bn Toman a year

33,641 bn lent over the year, 2.2 in every 1,000 not repaid. It assumes we recover nothing from a customer who stops paying, which is deliberately pessimistic — the operator keeps collecting after the line is cut off, so the real figure should come in under this.

4

Ignore the limit rule and it becomes about 124.9 bn

Customers whose bill doubles stop paying 1.7 times as often as customers whose bill rises by less. That multiple is the difference between the two figures, on exactly the same book and the same customers. It is the most expensive decision on this page and it costs nothing to get right.

What we do not know yet

Three things, said plainly, so nothing on this page is taken as more certain than it is.

Nobody in this data ever had credit

  • Every number here describes how customers behave when they are not borrowing. How they behave when they are is genuinely unknown.
  • A capped pilot is the only thing that answers it, and a one-month loan means the answer arrives in weeks rather than a year.

Does the loan add to the bill, or replace part of it?

  • If customers spend the loan on things they were going to buy anyway, their bill barely moves and the limit rule has room to spare.
  • If it is all extra spending, the bill rises by the full amount. Everything here assumes the second, which is the cautious reading.

What "stopped paying" means here

  • We count a customer as having stopped paying when their line is cut off in both directions. That is the operator's own decision and it takes time to reach — so some customers who are already in trouble have not been cut off yet when we count.
  • This makes the rates on this page a floor rather than a ceiling, and it is being checked separately. It does not change which customers are risky, only how many of them we have caught by the time we look.
