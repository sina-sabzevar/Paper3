Lending customers money on their next phone bill

A customer borrows against their phone bill, spends it on services they already use, and repays it on the bill that arrives next month. Every figure on this page was counted on our own customers in the last twelve months. There are no forecasts and no model — where something has not been measured, the page says so.

Read the loan sizes on this page as an upper bound

Every figure here is sized against what a customer spends with us each month. That is not what they are invoiced: roughly 48 pct of revenue is packages bought with cash, which never appear on the postpaid bill. A draw is repaid on the bill, so money that never reaches the bill cannot help repay it.

Sized against the bill instead, the number of customers who can take a 100,000 loan is roughly half what is shown below, and the 200,000 and 300,000 groups shrink further. The programme is likely around a third of the size on this page.

57_bill_basis.sql settles it. The billing system does hold the real figure — the end-of-cycle bill, which already excludes cash packages — and 57 rebuilds every count on it and measures the cash share on these exact customers instead of assuming a network average. The first check still does almost all the work — it removes 31,182,117 customers, 97 of every 100 we turn away. It is not a judgement about whether someone would repay, only about whether their bill can carry a loan at all.

How big the loan should be

This is the decision that sets the size of the whole programme, and it had never been counted until now.

Never lend someone more than they bill in a month.
When the loan is smaller than the usual bill, the bill goes up by less than double and customers carry on paying at close to the normal rate. Once the loan matches the bill, the bill doubles — and customers whose bill doubles stop paying 1.7 times as often. The line is sharp and it sits exactly at "the loan equals the bill".

What it is likely to cost

Worked out in the open so every step can be argued with.

1

6,438,513 customers borrow 180,883 Toman on average

That is 1,164.6 bn out on loan at any one time. The money comes back each month and goes out again, so 13,975 bn is lent over a full year.

2

About 1 in every 1,000 does not pay it back

Customers with no warning sign stopped paying at 3.1 in 1,000 over three months. A loan is only out for one month, so roughly a third of that applies to any single loan. This assumes the risk is spread evenly across the three months, which is a simplification.

3

So we lose roughly 16.4 bn Toman a year

It assumes we recover nothing from a customer who stops paying, which is deliberately pessimistic — the operator keeps collecting after the line is cut off. Against 13,975 bn lent, that is about 1.17 Toman lost for every 1,000 lent. Carrying through the year-on-year rise in cut-offs puts it at 22.3 bn.

4

A flat 300,000 loan would cost 62.1 bn instead

It lends more — 33,641 bn a year — but pushes 84 pct of customers into the band where people stop paying nearly twice as often. 3.8 times the losses for 2.4 times the lending. That is the whole argument for sizing the loan to the bill.

What we do not know yet

Four things, said plainly, so nothing above is taken as more certain than it is.

Nobody in this data ever had credit

  • Every number here describes how customers behave when they are not borrowing. How they behave when they are is genuinely unknown.
  • A capped pilot is the only thing that answers it, and a one-month loan means the answer arrives in weeks rather than a year.

We are measuring what they spend, not what they are invoiced

  • Every figure here uses total service revenue. About 48 pct of that is packages bought with cash and never reaches the postpaid bill, so the bill is roughly half what these numbers say.
  • A draw is repaid on the bill. Money that never reaches the bill cannot repay it, so the loan sizes here are too generous — every customer count is a ceiling, and the 2,906,210 we excluded for spending too little is an undercount.
  • There is one argument the other way and it should not be lost: the risk figures were measured against revenue too, both sides from the same column, so applying the rule in revenue terms is at least consistent with how they were fitted. The two readings disagree by about 1.9 times and we do not yet know which is right.
  • This does not have to be estimated. The billing system records the end-of-cycle bill, which already excludes cash packages. 57_bill_basis.sql rebuilds every count on it and measures the cash share on these exact customers rather than assuming the network average.

Which month the limit is set against

  • We use a month the customer reliably reaches — their second-lowest of six, 144,031 Toman for a typical customer.
  • Their six-month average is 262,860 Toman. Setting limits against the average instead would roughly double the programme and take on more risk. A decision, not a detail, and cheap to count both ways.

Does the loan add to the bill or replace part of it?

  • If customers spend it on things they were buying anyway, the bill barely moves and the limit rule has room to spare.
  • If it is all extra spending, the bill rises by the full amount. Everything here assumes the second, which is the cautious reading.

What "stopped paying" means here

  • A customer counts as having stopped paying when their line is cut off in both directions. That is the operator's own decision and it takes time to reach, so some customers already in trouble have not been cut off yet when we count.
  • That makes these rates a floor rather than a ceiling. It is being checked separately. It changes how many we have caught by the time we look, not which customers are risky.
